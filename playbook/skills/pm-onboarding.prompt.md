# PM Onboarding Expert

## Overview

A new PM's first 90 days are disproportionately important. Trust earned in the first quarter compounds; missteps in the first quarter haunt for the next year. This skill is a structured 30-60-90 day plan that helps a new PM diagnose the situation, build relationships, identify early wins, and arrive at the end of the quarter with credibility and a clear point of view.

It applies the STARS situation diagnosis (Start-up / Turnaround / Accelerated growth / Realignment / Sustaining success) to set how long to listen before acting.

### The arc in brief

- **Days 1-30 (Learn):** build context; no major decisions, no big PRDs. End with a 1-page "What I'm learning" memo (STARS diagnosis + top-3 going well + top-3 risks).
- **Days 31-60 (Plan):** form a point of view; interview customers yourself; identify 1-2 early wins; align manager + stakeholders. End with a first PRD/strategy memo and draft 6-month roadmap.
- **Days 61-90 (Deliver):** ship the early win; set the operating cadence; write the 90-day retro memo.

Diagnose the STARS situation first — a Sustaining-success area rewards 60+ days of listening; a Turnaround needs decisive action within 30. Misreading the situation is the most common new-PM mistake.

### When to Use

- **New job at a new company** -- use the full 90-day plan from week 1.
- **Internal transfer to a new team** -- compress to 60 days but keep the structure.
- **New scope within the same team** -- use the 30-day learning sprint only.
- **Returning from extended leave (3+ months)** -- use a modified 30-day plan to re-orient.

### When NOT to Use

- First week of a routine role (no major scope change) -- this is overkill.
- Interim leadership (acting role <60 days) -- run a stability-focused playbook instead.

## Clarify First

Before building the onboarding plan, settle the inputs below. When one is missing or vague and would change the result, ask about it; when it wouldn't, use a sensible default and say which one you used.

- **STARS situation:** Start-up, Turnaround, Accelerated-growth, Realignment, or Sustaining-success (sets the listen-vs-act balance; misreading it is the top new-PM mistake).
- **Transition type:** new company, internal transfer, new scope, or return from leave (chooses full-90 vs compressed-60 vs 30-day plan).
- **Manager's 90-day expectations:** what "success" means to them (anchors early-win selection and the whole arc).
- **Scope and key partners:** what the PM owns and who the critical stakeholders are (drives the stakeholder tiers and first-PRD).

Ask about the two or three inputs that matter most for this request, not the whole list. If the user wants a result straight away, produce it and state your assumptions at the top.

## Anti-Patterns

### Stakeholder map with no power dynamics
**Mistake:** The map lists names and titles and stops there.
**Why it happens:** An org chart is easy to copy, and opinions take conversations to learn.
**Instead:** For each stakeholder, record their interest (what they want from your team), their influence and their current sentiment. Use the map to decide who to meet first and who to align before whom.

### Saving the diagnosis for a Day-90 reveal
**Mistake:** You form strong views by Day 30 and keep them private until the 90-day memo.
**Why it happens:** Sharing a half-formed view feels riskier than presenting a finished one.
**Instead:** Share a strawman of your point of view around Day 45-60 and invite input. Before you publish, have at least three stakeholders pressure-test it.

### Overcommitting in the first sprint
**Mistake:** Keen to look productive, you take on eight deliverables in your first sprint and miss five.
**Why it happens:** New PMs want to show value fast, and they don't yet know how long things take here.
**Instead:** Under-promise for the first 60 days. Aim for a 100% hit rate on commitments in the first 30 days. Below 80% damages your reputation for the rest of the year.

## STARS: Diagnose the Situation Before Acting

Watkins' STARS framework forces you to ask: *what kind of situation am I entering?* The answer determines what early actions are appropriate.

| Type | What it means | First-90-day priorities |
|------|---------------|-------------------------|
| **S**tart-up | Building from scratch (new product, new team) | Move fast, hire, set direction, prove the idea |
| **T**urnaround | Failing product / team that needs rescue | Diagnose, cut, set a new direction, change personnel if needed |
| **A**ccelerated growth | Working, growing fast, breaking under scale | Build operating muscle, hire, design org for growth |
| **R**ealignment | Apparently successful but losing ground | Confront denial, align on the gap, make incremental change |
| **S**ustaining success | Working well, must be preserved and refined | Listen long before acting, do no harm, preserve trust |

**Why this matters:** A new PM walking into a Sustaining-success area should listen for at least 60 days. The same PM in a Turnaround should act within 30. Misreading the situation is the most common new-PM mistake.

### How to diagnose

In your first two weeks, ask every stakeholder:

1. "What is going well that you most want to preserve?"
2. "What is broken or breaking?"
3. "What changes would you most want from this role?"
4. "What would derail me in the first six months?"
5. "Who else should I be talking to about this?"

Patterns across 8-12 conversations reveal the STARS type.

## The 30-60-90 Day Plan

### Days 1-30: Learn

**Goal:** Build context. No major decisions. No big PRDs. Listen, observe, ask.

| Week | Focus | Outputs |
|------|-------|---------|
| Week 1 | Logistics, manager 1:1, team intros | Calendar set up, stakeholder shortlist, manager-aligned 90-day plan draft |
| Week 2 | Customer immersion: read research, listen to sales/support calls | Customer-segment map, top-5 customer pain hypotheses |
| Week 3 | Product immersion: use the product daily, read PRDs, walk the metrics tree | Product-area map, baseline metrics dashboard |
| Week 4 | Team immersion: 1:1 with every engineer and designer in the immediate team | Team strength/gap map, draft of STARS diagnosis |

**End-of-30 deliverable:** A 1-page "What I'm learning" memo to your manager. Includes your STARS diagnosis, top-3 things going well, top-3 risks, and questions you still need to answer.

### Days 31-60: Plan

**Goal:** Build a point of view. Identify the early-win candidates. Start to align stakeholders on direction.

| Week | Focus | Outputs |
|------|-------|---------|
| Week 5 | Deepen customer evidence: interview 3-5 customers yourself | Customer insight memo |
| Week 6 | Competitive scan + market context | Competitive 2x2 or summary |
| Week 7 | Identify 1-2 early wins (visible, achievable in <90 days) | Early-win proposal with cost/effort |
| Week 8 | Align manager + key stakeholders on early wins and 6-month direction | Aligned roadmap proposal |

**End-of-60 deliverable:** A first PRD or strategy memo for the early win, plus a draft 6-month roadmap. Share with manager and 2-3 key stakeholders for feedback before going wider.

### Days 61-90: Deliver

**Goal:** Deliver the early win. Set the operating cadence. Establish credibility.

| Week | Focus | Outputs |
|------|-------|---------|
| Week 9-10 | Ship the early win (or be in delivery with visible progress) | First shipped artifact under your name |
| Week 11 | Establish operating cadence: standups, planning, review, metrics | Cadence calendar published |
| Week 12 | Write the 90-day retro: what worked, what changed, what's next | 90-day retro memo |

**End-of-90 deliverable:** A retrospective memo. What you learned, what changed in your thinking from days 1-30, what your point of view is now, what the next 90 days look like.

## Early Wins: The Single Most Important Lever

In Watkins' framework, early wins are how new leaders build credibility. A good early win is:

- **Visible** -- People know it happened
- **Aligned** -- It moves something the org already cares about
- **Achievable** -- Within 90 days with available resources
- **Yours** -- Your fingerprints are on it (no claiming team-default work)

**Bad early wins:**

- A reorg in week 6 (too soon, no trust earned)
- A long-term strategy doc that lives on a wiki (not visible)
- "Cleaning up the backlog" (invisible to anyone but the team)

**Good early wins:**

- Shipping a previously-stalled feature
- Killing a project that everyone knew was zombie
- Running a customer research project whose results land in an exec readout
- Publishing a sharp competitive memo that becomes the team's reference

Identify candidates by week 6. Commit to one or two by week 8. Ship by week 12.

## Stakeholder Mapping for Onboarding

New-PM stakeholder mapping is different from steady-state mapping. The new PM is mapping *who knows what* and *whose trust matters*, not just *who has decision rights*.

Three-tier model:

| Tier | Description | Action |
|------|-------------|--------|
| Tier 1 | Direct manager, your team's eng/design lead, your 1-2 most-engaged cross-functional partners | Weekly 1:1 for first 90 days |
| Tier 2 | Skip-level, adjacent PM peers, key cross-functional stakeholders (sales, support, legal, marketing) | One 1:1 within first 30 days, monthly afterward |
| Tier 3 | Anyone else useful for context (former PM of the area, customer success leads, exec sponsors) | One 1:1 within first 60 days |

Aim for **25-35 distinct conversations** in the first 60 days. Less is too little context; more is performance.

See `assets/stakeholder_map.md` for the full template.

## The First-PRD Trap

New PMs often want to author a big PRD in week 4 to "make their mark". Don't. A first PRD has unique requirements:

1. **Smaller than you think.** Pick a tight scope you can ship inside the 90-day window.
2. **Heavily co-authored.** Engineering and design leads should review the first draft before week 6.
3. **Customer-evidenced.** Cite 2-3 customer interviews you ran yourself.
4. **Aligned to existing strategy.** Don't propose a left-turn in your first PRD; do that in months 6-9.
5. **Short.** A great first PRD is 2-4 pages, not 10.

See `assets/first_prd_template.md`.

## Workflow

1. **Pre-start (1-2 weeks before day 1).** Read public materials about the company, product, market. Reach out to your future manager for a 30-minute call.
2. **Day 1.** Get logistics done. Send a "what to expect from me / what I'd love from you" intro note to the team.
3. **Week 1.** Set up your stakeholder map. Schedule 1:1s with everyone in Tier 1. Get on customer calls.
4. **Week 2-4.** Run the 30-day learning sprint. Resist the urge to make big proposals.
5. **End of week 4.** Send the "What I'm learning" memo to your manager.
6. **Week 5-8.** Build the point of view. Identify and align on early wins.
7. **Week 9-12.** Deliver the early win. Set the cadence.
8. **End of week 12.** Send the 90-day retro memo. Schedule the cycle-1 check-in with your manager.

## Troubleshooting

| Symptom | Likely Cause | Resolution |
|---------|-------------|------------|
| You feel pressure to "make a mark" in week 2 | Manager or skip-level signaled urgency early | Renegotiate the timeline; share Watkins' early-win principle; commit to a 90-day visible outcome rather than week-2 declarations |
| You inherit a roadmap you don't agree with | Predecessor committed to work before you arrived | Honor existing commitments through your learning period; propose adjustments in week 8 with evidence, not in week 2 with opinion |
| Stakeholders give you conflicting accounts of "what's broken" | Normal in months 1-2; reveals organizational tensions | Triangulate across 8-12 conversations before forming a view; the truth is usually the intersection |
| You haven't met your skip-level by week 4 | Calendar friction; new PM hesitant to ask | Ask your manager to introduce or directly request 30 minutes; skip-level visibility in the first 60 days is essential |
| You spend all your time in meetings and can't think | Over-scheduled in the learning phase | Block 2-hour daily focus windows; reduce 1:1 cadence to 30 min where possible; protect a "no meetings" half-day per week |
| Your STARS diagnosis is wrong (e.g., you thought Turnaround but it's Sustaining success) | Misread early signals; missed long-tenured staff voices | Re-interview 3-5 senior people in week 6; explicitly ask "what should I not change in my first year?"; revise diagnosis |
| The early win you committed to is at risk of slipping | Over-scoped or hit unexpected dependencies | Cut scope ruthlessly; ship a smaller version on time rather than a bigger version late; the timeline is the win, not the size |
| You haven't sent the 90-day retro because it feels self-indulgent | Imposter syndrome around "what did I actually do" | Send it anyway; it forces clarity, signals to your manager that you self-reflect, and creates the artifact you'll need for year-end review |

## Success Criteria

- By end of week 1: stakeholder map drafted, Tier 1 1:1s scheduled
- By end of week 4: "What I'm learning" memo delivered to manager
- By end of week 8: 25-35 stakeholder conversations completed; STARS diagnosis confirmed; early-win candidates aligned
- By end of week 12: early win shipped or in visible delivery; operating cadence published; 90-day retro memo delivered
- By end of 90 days: your manager can articulate your point of view in 2 sentences; at least 3 cross-functional partners would proactively recommend working with you
- Your first PRD is short, customer-evidenced, and on the smaller end of scope you considered

## references/first-90-days-playbook.md

# First 90 Days Playbook for PMs

A Watkins-grounded deep dive on the principles that drive successful PM transitions.

---

## 1. The transition trap

Most PMs are evaluated on their first quarter even though they have the least context in their first quarter. The trap: feeling you must "produce" before you have the evidence to know what to produce.

Watkins' core principle is that **the first 90 days are about achieving the breakeven point** -- the point at which the value you've added equals the cost of bringing you in. Acting before reaching breakeven (declaring strategy, reorging, killing things) burns trust faster than it builds.

The structured 30-60-90 plan exists to compress the time-to-breakeven while protecting against premature action.

---

## 2. STARS in depth

STARS is not academic. Each type has distinct rules for what early actions are appropriate.

### Start-up

**Looks like:** New product, new team, no existing customers (or very few). Things you build from scratch.

**Pitfalls:** Hesitating to make decisions because "we don't have data yet". At start-up, you must form a point of view from limited data and iterate fast.

**90-day priorities:**
- Set direction (vision, customer hypothesis)
- Hire the right initial team
- Build the smallest thing that proves the idea
- Establish operating cadence early

### Turnaround

**Looks like:** A failing product or team. Metrics declining, morale low, leadership has lost confidence.

**Pitfalls:** "Listening tour" too long. At turnaround, the team needs visible decisive action within 30 days or trust erodes further.

**90-day priorities:**
- Diagnose quickly (intense first 2 weeks)
- Cut what is not working (projects, possibly people)
- Set a new direction
- Pick a high-symbolism early win

### Accelerated Growth

**Looks like:** Working product, breaking under scale. Hiring fast, processes lag, quality slips.

**Pitfalls:** Trying to "fix everything" in the first 90 days. At accelerated growth, the work is operating-system design, not heroics.

**90-day priorities:**
- Strengthen operating cadence
- Hire the right roles for scale
- Identify the 2-3 metrics that matter most
- Resist the urge to introduce too much process at once

### Realignment

**Looks like:** Apparently successful, but losing share or relevance. Most insidious -- the team believes things are fine.

**Pitfalls:** Aggressive moves alienate the team that built the current success. At realignment, the work is changing minds before changing actions.

**90-day priorities:**
- Confront the denial (data-driven evidence the team can see)
- Build alignment around the gap
- Make incremental, evidence-driven changes
- Identify the small set of allies for the longer transformation

### Sustaining Success

**Looks like:** Working well, market-leading. Your job is to not break it.

**Pitfalls:** "Make your mark" pressure leads to changing things that didn't need changing. At sustaining success, the most experienced employees are the ones whose trust matters most.

**90-day priorities:**
- Listen long (60+ days)
- Identify the 2-3 levers for marginal improvement
- Preserve what works
- Build trust with long-tenured staff

---

## 3. Securing early wins

A correctly chosen early win:

1. **Is visible to your manager and skip-level** -- They can name it 90 days from now.
2. **Is achievable inside 90 days** -- Or has visible meaningful progress by day 90.
3. **Aligns with existing priorities** -- Don't pick a contrarian early win.
4. **Has clear authorship** -- Your fingerprints are unambiguous.
5. **Builds long-term capability** -- Bonus if the win sets up later wins (e.g., a customer research cadence, a metric dashboard, a documentation hub).

### Pattern-library of strong early wins for PMs

- Ship a previously-stalled feature with renewed engineering buy-in
- Kill a zombie project and free engineering capacity
- Run a customer research project whose findings appear in an exec readout
- Publish a sharp competitive memo that becomes the team's reference
- Author a re-prioritized backlog with explicit rationale that earns engineering trust
- Introduce a regular metrics review that surfaces an issue the team didn't know about
- Resolve a long-standing cross-functional tension (e.g., handoff between PM and Marketing)

### Pattern-library of weak early wins

- Reorging the team in week 6 (no earned trust)
- A strategy doc that never leaves a wiki
- "Cleaning up the backlog" (invisible)
- A new framework / template proliferation (process for process's sake)
- A win owned 50/50 with a peer (no clear authorship)

---

## 4. Negotiating success with your manager

Watkins emphasizes the **five-conversations framework** -- five distinct conversations with your manager in the first 90 days:

| Conversation | Timing | What to discuss |
|--------------|--------|-----------------|
| 1. Situational diagnosis | Week 1-2 | What kind of STARS situation am I in? Do we agree? |
| 2. Expectations | Week 2-4 | What does success at 6 months look like? At 12 months? |
| 3. Style | Week 3-4 | How does my manager prefer to work? Frequency, format, escalation thresholds? |
| 4. Resources | Week 4-8 | What do I need to succeed? People, budget, decision rights? |
| 5. Personal development | Week 6-8 | What does my manager see as my development priorities? |

These do not have to be 5 separate meetings. They should all happen by week 8.

---

## 5. Achieving alignment in the team

By day 90, the new PM should have:

- A shared point of view with the team about *what we're trying to accomplish*
- A shared point of view with the team about *how we operate* (rituals, decision rights, communication)
- A shared point of view with the team about *what's broken and what we're fixing first*

Alignment is built through repeated conversation, not declared in a kickoff. The week-by-week 1:1s, the early-win project, and the 90-day retro memo are the artifacts that build alignment over time.

---

## 6. Common new-PM failure modes

| Failure mode | Why it happens | How to avoid |
|--------------|----------------|---------------|
| Spend 90 days in listen mode, never acts | Imposter syndrome; "I need more data" | Commit to an early win by day 60; ship by day 90 |
| Declare strategy in week 4 | "Make your mark" pressure | Watkins' rule: wait for breakeven before strategic moves |
| Inherits and accepts the existing roadmap unchanged | Conflict avoidance | Use weeks 5-8 to build a point of view and propose adjustments with evidence |
| Builds a 12-page PRD as the first deliverable | Demonstrating "thoroughness" | First PRD: 2-4 pages, customer-evidenced, scoped to ship in 90 days |
| Avoids difficult conversations with cross-functional partners | Wants to be liked | Address tensions in the first 60 days; tensions in months 1-2 are easier than month 6 |
| Doesn't meet skip-level until month 4 | Calendar friction; deference | Request the skip-level 1:1 in week 2 |
| Sends no written updates to manager | "My manager knows what I'm doing" | Weekly written status in first 90 days; even a 5-line note |
| Skips the 90-day retro | "Self-evaluation feels weird" | Send it anyway; it's the most important artifact of the cycle |

---

## 7. Onboarding when remote or hybrid

The 90-day playbook applies, with adjustments:

- **More 1:1s, shorter:** 30 min beats 60 min when virtual; you can do more per week
- **Written communication weighs more:** Async updates, public docs, recorded meeting summaries
- **Be in the office for "high-bandwidth" moments:** Quarterly planning, executive reviews, customer visits
- **Proactively schedule informal time:** 15-min coffee chats with peers; remote work eliminates accidental face time

---

## 8. The 90-day retro memo

Structure for the end-of-cycle memo to your manager:

1. **What I learned about the STARS situation** -- has your diagnosis changed?
2. **Top 3 things going well** that I want to preserve
3. **Top 3 risks** that need attention
4. **My point of view on the area** -- 1-2 paragraphs of original synthesis
5. **What I shipped / impacted** -- the early win and any other tangible outcomes
6. **What I'd do differently** -- self-aware reflection on missteps
7. **What I need from you** -- specific manager support for the next quarter
8. **Next 90 days** -- top priorities going into the second cycle

This memo is the artifact that locks in the credibility built over the first quarter. Send it. Don't skip it.

# Red Flags: PM Onboarding

> Common ways this skill's output goes wrong — concrete examples, why they're bad, and how to fix them. Pair with the SKILL.md and Troubleshooting table.

## How to use this document

When you have just produced a 30-60-90 day plan, stakeholder map, or onboarding artifact, scan the red flags below. Each red flag shows a *bad* and *good* version of an early-tenure decision.

---

## Red Flag 1: Jumping to Delivery Before Earning the Right to Lead

**Symptom.** Day 12 of the new role, the PM is already proposing a roadmap change, a re-org, or a "fix" for what they perceive as a broken process.

**Why it's bad.** Watkins' first 90 days exist for diagnosis. A PM who proposes changes before listening will be ignored by stakeholders who do not yet trust them, and will probably be wrong because they have not yet seen the iceberg under the surface.

**Bad example:**
> Week 2 in role: "I've put together a new roadmap that drops three of the active initiatives. Can we discuss in tomorrow's product trio?"

**Good example:**
> Week 2 in role: "I've shadowed 4 customer calls, read the last 6 months of PRDs, and had 1:1s with 7 stakeholders. Themes so far: [...]. I'd like to spend the next two weeks deepening before proposing changes. First strawman thoughts by end of Day 30."

**How to catch it.** Before any change proposal in the first 30 days, ask: "have I done at least 10 hours of listening on this topic?" If not, hold the proposal.

---

## Red Flag 2: STARS Misdiagnosis

**Symptom.** PM treats a Turnaround as Sustaining Success (low urgency, light touch), or treats a Sustaining Success as a Turnaround (sweeping changes, frame as broken).

**Why it's bad.** STARS (Watkins) prescribes very different actions per situation. A turnaround needs decisive early moves; a sustaining-success role needs deference and care. Misdiagnosing the situation produces actions that backfire — fast changes in a healthy team alienate it, slow listening in a turnaround team prolongs the bleeding.

**Bad example:**
> Team is in Turnaround (key engineer left, two missed launches, declining metrics). PM enters with "let me listen for 90 days before changing anything."

**Good example:**
> Team is in Turnaround. PM's first 30 days: (a) confirm diagnosis with skip-level + 5 stakeholders, (b) pick *one* visible early win to deliver by Day 45 (stabilize releases, hit a near-term commitment), (c) protect the team from new asks for 30 days while triage happens.

**How to catch it.** Have you written down your STARS classification with evidence? If not, you cannot have calibrated your action pace.

---

## Red Flag 3: Stakeholder Map Missing Power Dynamics

**Symptom.** Stakeholder map lists names and titles. Does not show influence, interests, current opinion of the team, or relationship to other stakeholders.

**Why it's bad.** A map without power dynamics is an org chart. The point of the stakeholder map is to inform sequencing — who to meet first, who to align before whom, where political risks live. A flat list will get the sequencing wrong.

**Bad example:**
> Stakeholder map: "Sales VP — Jane. Eng VP — Raj. Marketing VP — Sam. Design Director — Liz."

**Good example:**
> Stakeholder map: "Sales VP Jane (high influence, frustrated with last quarter's slip; meet first, listen, get 1 win). Eng VP Raj (high influence, supportive of incoming PM, allies with Sam). Marketing VP Sam (medium influence, neutral). Design Director Liz (medium influence, key product partner; meet by Day 10)."

**How to catch it.** For each stakeholder, can you state their *interest* (what they want from your team), *influence*, and *current sentiment*? If not, the map is incomplete.

---

## Red Flag 4: 30-60-90 Plan with Identical Activities Each Phase

**Symptom.** The plan says "do customer interviews" in all three phases. "Build stakeholder relationships" appears at Day 30, 60, and 90.

**Why it's bad.** The 30-60-90 structure exists because the phases serve different purposes: 30 = listen and diagnose, 60 = test hypotheses and early wins, 90 = commit to direction. A plan that repeats activities loses the phase logic and produces a 90-day plateau instead of a 90-day climb.

**Bad example:**
> "Day 1-30: meet stakeholders, learn product. Day 31-60: meet stakeholders, learn product. Day 61-90: meet stakeholders, learn product."

**Good example:**
> "Day 1-30 (Listen): 1:1s with all stakeholders, 5 customer interviews, read PRDs/postmortems, shadow support. Day 31-60 (Test): write strawman point-of-view memo, propose 1 hypothesis-testing experiment, deliver 1 small early win. Day 61-90 (Commit): publish 90-day POV memo, propose Q+1 priorities, build buy-in."

**How to catch it.** Each phase should have distinct output artifacts. If the artifacts repeat, the phases blur.

---

## Red Flag 5: No Early Win Identified

**Symptom.** The 90-day plan has no concrete "deliverable by Day 45" — just learning activities.

**Why it's bad.** Trust is built by delivery, not by listening. A new PM who completes 90 days of learning with no visible win is judged "still ramping" indefinitely. The early win does not need to be large — it needs to be visible to the team and stakeholders.

**Bad example:**
> "First 90 days will be focused on learning and listening."

**Good example:**
> "Early win target by Day 45: ship the bug-triage process the team has wanted for 3 months. Small in scope, big in signal — shows I listen, deliver, and reduce friction."

**How to catch it.** What is your Day-45 visible deliverable? If you cannot name it, the plan lacks an early win.

---

## Red Flag 6: Calendar Filled with Internal Meetings, No Customer Contact

**Symptom.** Week 4. PM has had 22 internal meetings and 0 customer conversations.

**Why it's bad.** Internal meetings are a comfort zone — same vocabulary, friendly faces, easy debriefs. Customer conversations are uncomfortable but produce the only outside-in data. A PM who has not heard 5 customers in 30 days will absorb the internal narrative and lose product judgment.

**Bad example:**
> Week 4: 22 internal 1:1s, 0 customer calls, 0 support tickets read.

**Good example:**
> Week 4: 12 internal 1:1s, 6 customer calls (3 won, 3 churned), shadowed 4 support tickets, watched 10 session replays. Patterns emerging: [...]"

**How to catch it.** Track ratio of internal-to-external time. If below 5:1 in the first 30 days, you are absorbing the internal bubble.

---

## Red Flag 7: First-PRD Used to Make a Statement

**Symptom.** New PM's first PRD is a major scope, intended to "establish credibility." It is over-engineered, takes a month to write, and signals overreach.

**Why it's bad.** The first PRD's job is to show *how you work*, not to land a marquee win. A first PRD that tries to be the most important PRD of the year almost always lands wrong — too big, too soon, too dependent on relationships that have not been built.

**Bad example:**
> First PRD: 18-page rewrite of the team's entire activation strategy, submitted Week 6.

**Good example:**
> First PRD: a small but well-formed feature in the current sprint, written in the team's template. Shows clear thinking, calibrated scope, partnership with EM and designer. Lands by Week 4.

**How to catch it.** Is your first PRD small enough to ship in the current cycle? If it is multi-quarter, you are over-reaching.

---

## Red Flag 8: Skip-Level Relationship Neglected

**Symptom.** PM has had multiple 1:1s with their manager but never met their skip-level past introductions.

**Why it's bad.** The skip-level (your manager's manager) is the person who will sponsor your promotion, sign off on your strategy memo, and decide whether you are a "rising star" or "still ramping." Skip-level relationships compound; starting at Day 90 is too late.

**Bad example:**
> Day 60. Skip-level introduction was Day 1, no contact since.

**Good example:**
> Day 60. Two skip-level touchpoints: (a) Day 20, manager-arranged 30-min coffee with explicit "what do you want from me" framing, (b) Day 55, sent strawman POV memo for input ahead of formal Day-90 publication."

**How to catch it.** When did your skip-level last hear from you, and what did they hear? If never substantively, the relationship is shallow.

---

## Red Flag 9: Diagnosis Sealed in Private (No Strawman Shared)

**Symptom.** PM has formed strong opinions by Day 30 but keeps them private until the "big reveal" Day-90 memo.

**Why it's bad.** A 90-day surprise memo is high risk. Stakeholders feel they were not consulted; the memo is critiqued for what it missed (which they would have flagged earlier). The fix is to share a *strawman* version at Day 45-60 and invite input.

**Bad example:**
> Day 30-89: PM gathers data silently. Day 90: publishes 8-page POV memo with major recommendations.

**Good example:**
> Day 45: PM shares 1-page strawman POV with manager + skip-level + 3 key stakeholders. Day 60: revised strawman shared with broader team for input. Day 90: published POV is the *third draft*, with broad buy-in earned along the way."

**How to catch it.** Has your POV been pressure-tested by 3+ stakeholders before publication? If not, you are betting the launch on private analysis.

---

## Red Flag 10: Overcommitting in the First Sprint

**Symptom.** New PM, eager to look productive, takes on 8 deliverables in the first sprint, misses 5 of them.

**Why it's bad.** First-quarter signal is loud. A new PM who misses commitments early loses credibility for the rest of the year. The right move is to under-promise and over-deliver in the first 60 days — your future reputation depends on this calibration.

**Bad example:**
> Week 2 commitments: deliver onboarding revamp PRD, run 10 customer interviews, propose new prioritization framework, lead next QBR. Result: 2 of 4 delivered.

**Good example:**
> Week 2 commitments: 5 customer interviews, sit in on 2 sprint planning sessions, deliver a 1-page summary of findings by Week 4. Result: all 3 delivered + early win opportunity surfaced."

**How to catch it.** What is your delivery rate on commitments in the first 30 days? Aim for 100%. Under 80% damages reputation.

---

## Red Flag 11: Treating the Predecessor's Work as Wrong by Default

**Symptom.** New PM frames previous decisions as mistakes, plans, or roadmaps to undo. Visible to the team.

**Why it's bad.** Even if previous decisions were wrong, framing them as such alienates the team that made them and signals the new PM is more interested in differentiation than continuity. Watkins' Sustaining-Success scenarios specifically warn against this pattern.

**Bad example:**
> Week 4: "The previous PM's roadmap missed the most important opportunity. Here's a new direction."

**Good example:**
> Week 4: "The previous roadmap delivered [X] which gave us [Y]. Based on what I'm hearing, the next move I'd consider building on that is [Z]. Curious what the team thinks."

**How to catch it.** Have you publicly criticized any decision made before you arrived? If yes, walk it back; the team made those decisions and you damaged trust.

---

## Red Flag 12: No Personal Pace / Burnout in First Quarter

**Symptom.** New PM works 11-hour days, weekends, eats lunch at desk, skips exercise. Burns out by Day 60.

**Why it's bad.** First 90 days are a marathon. Burning out at Day 60 means the most important quarter ends in apology, not impact. Stamina is a deliverable.

**Bad example:**
> Week 6: PM working 70+ hour weeks, has missed family commitments, irritable on Slack, no longer sleeping well.

**Good example:**
> Week 6: PM works hard but maintains sleep, exercise, and at least 1 family/personal evening per week. Has said no to 2 nice-to-have asks. Stamina is intact through Day 90 and beyond."

**How to catch it.** At Week 6, do you have the energy reserve for the next 6 weeks? If not, scale back commitments now, not at Week 12.
